1. WHY THESE LAWS MATTER TO OUR TOOL
------------------------------------
Our tool works because of one idea in modern privacy law: people have the
right to get a copy of the data a company holds about them.

That right is the reason "Download your data" buttons exist (Google Takeout,
Instagram download, Spotify export). Our tool takes those downloads, reads
them on the user's own device, and makes them understandable.

Three rights matter most to us:
  - Right of access:        "Show me what you hold about me."
  - Right of portability:   "Give me my data in a file I can reuse."
  - Right of deletion:      "Remove my data."
Our tool helps people use all three, because the report tells them what to
delete and how.

3. THE LAWS, ONE BY ONE
-----------------------

3.1 GDPR - European Union (in force since 25 May 2018)
  What it is:  The most influential privacy law in the world. It applies to
               any organisation handling data of people in the EU, wherever
               the organisation is based.
  Key rights:  Access (Article 15), correction, deletion, restriction,
               objection, and portability (Article 20).
  Portability: The person can receive the data they provided, in a
               structured, commonly used, machine-readable format, and can
               pass it to another service.
  Breaches:    Authorities must normally be told within 72 hours.
  Penalties:   Up to EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide yearly revenue,
               whichever is higher.
  Link to us:  GDPR is the reason big platforms give downloads at all. It
               is the benchmark many other laws copy.

3.2 UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 - United Kingdom
  What it is:  The UK's version of GDPR after Brexit. Rights are very
               similar to the EU's.
  Regulator:   The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO).
  Link to us:  Same rights, so the same tool works for UK users.

3.3 DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025 - India
  What it is:  India's first full data protection law.
  Key terms:   "Data Principal" = the person. "Data Fiduciary" = the
               company holding the data. Data Protection Board of India
               handles complaints.
  Rights:      Get information about processing, correction and erasure,
               grievance redressal, and nominating someone to act for you.
  Penalties:   Up to INR 250 crore for serious violations.
  Status:      Phased rollout. The Rules were notified on 13 November
               2025. Rules on Consent Managers start in November 2026. The
               main duties for companies (notice, consent, safeguards)
               start on 13 May 2027.
  Limits:      Critics point to wide exemptions for government bodies.
               The Act is built around consent and does not spell out a
               portability right in the way GDPR does.
  Link to us:  Our home market. Most Indian users do not yet know their
               rights, which is exactly the gap our tool addresses.

3.4 CCPA / CPRA - California, USA
  What it is:  California's privacy law. Gives residents rights to know
               what is collected, to delete, to correct, and to opt out of
               the sale or sharing of their data.
  Also covers: Limiting use of sensitive personal information.
  Link to us:  Shows how privacy rights work in a market with no single
               national law.

3.5 Other US state laws
  What it is:  The US has no single national privacy law. Sector laws
               (such as HIPAA for health) and many state laws fill the
               space. Reports in 2026 count more than 20 state laws,
               including Virginia (VCDPA) and Colorado (CPA).
  Detail:      Many state laws copy GDPR's wording on portability but not
               exactly. Some limit portability to data the consumer
               provided.
  Link to us:  Rules differ state by state, so a simple, local tool that
               works from any export is useful.

3.6 LGPD - Brazil
  What it is:  Brazil's general data protection law, closely modelled on
               GDPR.
  Rights:      Access, correction, portability, deletion, and clear
               consent.
  Detail:      One older comparison noted a 15-day response time to
               requests, shorter than GDPR's usual one month. Check the
               current text before quoting this.
  Link to us:  Another large market with portability built in.

3.7 PIPEDA - Canada
  What it is:  Canada's federal private-sector privacy law.
  Rights:      Access and correction.
  Portability: Not part of the current law. Canada has been studying a
               portability approach.
  Link to us:  Users in Canada can ask for their data, but portability is
               still under discussion.

3.8 Privacy Act 1988 - Australia
  What it is:  Australia's main privacy law.
  Rights:      Access and correction (Australian Privacy Principles 12
               and 13).
  Portability: No general portability right. Australia has a separate
               Consumer Data Right (CDR) that covers specific sectors, and
               it is being reset and expanded.
  Link to us:  Shows a model where portability comes from sector rules.

3.9 PIPL - China
  What it is:  China's personal information protection law.
  Portability: Includes a portability right with conditions, such as
               verifying the person's identity and having a suitable legal
               basis for the processing.

3.10 Malaysia PDPA
  Portability: A formal right to data portability came into force on
               1 June 2025 as part of the third phase of changes. It
               depends on technical feasibility and compatible formats.

3.11 POPIA - South Africa, and others
  What it is:  South Africa's data protection law with access, correction
               and deletion rights. Singapore (PDPA), South Korea (PIPA)
               and Japan (APPI) also have personal data laws. South Korea
               has consulted on broader portability.


4. NEWER RULES THAT GO BEYOND GDPR PORTABILITY
----------------------------------------------
  - EU Digital Markets Act (DMA): Pushes large "gatekeeper" platforms to
    give stronger data portability, built on competition goals rather than
    pure privacy.
  - EU Data Act: Adds rules on access to data generated by connected
    products and services.
  - US ACCESS Act (proposed bill): A competition-focused portability
    proposal.
  Link to us: The trend is more and easier data export. That means more
  raw data landing in people's hands, which makes a tool like ours more
  useful over time.


5. THE GAP THAT OUR PROJECT FILLS
---------------------------------
a) Rights exist but people cannot use them.
   Laws give access and portability, but exports arrive as ZIP files full
   of JSON that normal people cannot read.

b) Inferred data is a grey area.
   Research on US state laws notes that portability often covers data the
   person provided, and it is unclear whether it covers inferences the
   company made about them (for example, "interested in loans").
   Access rights are generally broader than portability rights.
   This is why our "what they guessed about you" section is important:
   it shows people data they may never have seen.

c) No single view across companies.
   Each platform shows only its own data. Our tool combines them.

d) Privacy needs to be provable.
   Many "checker" tools ask users to upload their data. Ours reads the ZIP
   on the user's device, so the user does not have to trust another
   company.


6. HOW TO USE THIS IN THE PITCH
-------------------------------
  - One line: "Laws like GDPR and India's DPDP Act gave people the right to
    their data. We make that right usable."
  - Point out that more than one country now has these rights, so the
    problem is global, not only Indian.
  - Be honest about timing: India's main DPDP duties start on 13 May 2027,
    so platform settings are the fastest protection today.
  - Mention the future feature: generate a ready-to-send access or
    deletion request under the user's local law.
  - Do not claim to give legal advice. The tool explains data and suggests
    steps; it is not a lawyer.
